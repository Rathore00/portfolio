Case Study · American Express GBT · HRE Hotel Reshop

Making $107M in savings
visible

HRE had been quietly re-booking hotel reservations at lower rates for years. The engine worked. The problem was that no one could see how — or where — it was working.

Role
Product Designer
Contribution
Individual contributor
Timeline
2–4 months
Status
Development in progress
Context

An engine running without a dashboard

HRE is an automated system that monitors hotel bookings and re-books them when a lower rate becomes available — generating significant savings for GBT's corporate travel clients at scale. Despite this, there was no analytics surface built to track or communicate how the engine was performing.

Stakeholders were managing a growing operation through spreadsheets and a partial, largely non-functional Power BI dashboard. The business was expanding, the data was piling up, and the tools weren't keeping pace. Leadership had no reliable way to distinguish between monitoring the system and responding to it.

This project was brought in to fix that — from scratch, solo, with no dedicated research function and a data landscape that was still evolving.

Problem

Too much data. No shared understanding of what to act on.

The core problem wasn't a lack of data — it was an absence of structure around it. Stakeholders were digging through exports and half-built views to answer questions that should have been immediately surfaced. Each team came with its own interpretation of what mattered, and there was no shared frame of reference.

The business had a clear story to tell. It just had no way of telling it — to itself, or to the people responsible for growing it.

Two things compounded this: 34 KPIs were in scope, with no alignment on priority, and some of the underlying data was still raw and uncategorised. Any solution would need to handle ambiguity — in the data, in the requirements, and in the stakeholders' own understanding of what they needed.

Research & Discovery

Starting with the data, because the brief couldn't

With no research function attached to the project, the starting point was the data itself. Systematic analysis of the existing dataset surfaced three patterns that hadn't been articulated in any brief or stakeholder conversation: a clear growth trajectory in reshop volume, periodic spikes in rejection rates tied to specific supplier behaviour, and a concentration of savings in a narrow band of bookings.

These weren't minor observations — they shaped the entire information architecture that followed. The data told a story that no one had yet put into words.

Finding 01
Growth story

Reshop volume was increasing at a rate that the existing tooling couldn't meaningfully track or communicate.

Finding 02
Rejection spikes

Periodic rejections correlated with specific supplier behaviour — a pattern invisible in the existing dashboard.

Finding 03
Savings concentration

A narrow band of bookings accounted for a disproportionate share of savings — useful for prioritisation.

Alongside data analysis, repeated working sessions with stakeholders helped identify not what data existed, but what decisions stakeholders were trying to make — and what they'd do differently with better visibility. This distinction drove the eventual KPI groupings.

One significant investment was domain learning: hotel reshop has its own terminology, supplier dynamics, and operational logic. Without at least a working understanding of what a "rejection" or "reshop attempt" actually meant in context, the design work would have been surface-level at best. The domain ramp-up extended the timeline — but it made every subsequent decision more grounded.

Strategic Framing

Not all 34 KPIs serve the same purpose

The central tension throughout the project: stakeholders wanted all 34 KPIs visible and equally accessible. That's a reasonable ask — each metric has an owner, and each owner has a use for it. But presenting 34 signals with equal weight means designing for no one in particular.

The position held throughout: equal weight is not neutral design. It's a choice that pushes cognitive load onto the user and makes the most important signals harder to find, not easier.

  • Principle

    Numbers over charts where the difference margin between data points is low enough that a visual encoding creates ambiguity rather than clarity.

  • Principle

    Clarity over aesthetics throughout. This is a working tool — its job is to inform decisions, not to demonstrate visual craft.

  • Principle

    Organise by attention type, not by data structure. The three-tab model reflects how stakeholders actually engage with the product: orientation, investigation, action.

The pushback on equal-weight KPI presentation was documented and defended across multiple reviews. The goal wasn't to remove KPIs — it was to give them appropriate context based on when and why someone would need them.

Design Process

Structuring 34 KPIs without overwhelming the people who use them

The design challenge was structural before it was visual: how do you organise a large, partially-raw dataset into something a stakeholder can act on in under a minute?

Earlier explorations tried a single scrollable view with progressive disclosure. Stakeholder feedback was consistent — the cognitive cost of orientation was too high. People needed to know which mode they were in before engaging with individual numbers.

The three-tab model emerged from that feedback. Each tab has a clear job. Nothing is hidden — the hierarchy is in the entry point, not the data itself.

Summary
Insights
Attention
Summary

Executive orientation. Headline reshop volume, savings rate, and system-level health at a glance. Designed for speed — no drilling required.

Insights

Operational depth for teams managing supplier performance and reshop efficiency. Where patterns and trends live.

Attention

Proactively surfaced anomalies and metrics that require action — rather than leaving stakeholders to discover them in a data scan.

Throughout this phase, stakeholder sessions ran in parallel — validating that the tab groupings reflected how teams actually thought about the business, and managing expectations around which KPIs were ready to surface given the state of the underlying data. Some high-value metrics weren't reliable enough to show yet. Those were flagged explicitly, not quietly dropped.

Outcomes

A working tool, and follow-on work that says more than metrics

The design phase is complete. Development is in progress. Post-launch data isn't available yet — which is the honest picture.

What the dashboard delivers is a structured, reliable surface that replaces ad-hoc spreadsheet analysis and a non-functional Power BI view. For the first time, stakeholders across the HRE business have a shared starting point: a place to orient quickly, investigate when needed, and act on what matters today.

The more meaningful signal is this: stakeholders who engaged with this work came back with new project requirements. That's not a metric — but it's evidence that the value of the work was understood. A dashboard that gets handed off and never questioned usually means one of two things. This felt like the other one.

Reflection

What held, what didn't, and what's still open

Do differently

Push harder for a shared requirements document in week one. Stakeholder input arrived in fragments — a structured alignment session early on would have compressed the timeline without sacrificing the depth of the conversations that followed.

The hard call

Equal-weight KPI display was the stakeholder ask. It wasn't the right solution. That position held — but it was relitigated more than it needed to be. Documenting the rationale formally, earlier, would have shortened those conversations.

What surprised

The three-tab structure came from stakeholder conversations, not the data. The data revealed what existed. The people revealed what needed to be findable — and in what order.

Still open

Whether the dashboard shifts stakeholder behaviour — faster decisions, fewer ad-hoc data requests — isn't measurable yet. That's the real impact question, and it belongs to what comes after launch.

Stand behind

The numbers-over-charts principle for low-margin comparisons, and the separation of summary / insight / action. Both were debated. Neither changed. Both were right for this product and this audience.
